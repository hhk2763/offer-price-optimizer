The problem

The client buys surplus aircraft parts, usually in AR As Removed condition. They pay a repair station to overhaul each one, then sell it as OH Overhauled or SV Serviceable. Before any offer goes out, each part in a lot needs the same answer: what is the most we can pay and still hit our number?

That answer lived in Google Sheets. Each deal had its own workbook with eight to ten tabs: the seller's parts list, sourcing quotes, repair-station quotes, supplier directories, and a summary sheet held together with lookups. Pricing one part meant cross-referencing three tabs and doing the arithmetic by hand. The team put a full deal at six to nine hours of work.

How a deal makes money

Three steps, three numbers.

1 · Buy

Part arrives AR

Removed from an aircraft, condition unknown until inspected.

You pay: offer
2 · Overhaul

Repair station

Inspection fee, then overhaul or repair. Quotes vary widely by shop and lead time.

You pay: OH cost
3 · Sell

Leaves OH or SV

Tagged with an airworthiness release (FAA 8130-3 or EASA Form 1), sold outright or on exchange.

You earn: sale price
Gross profit = Sale price − Offer − OH cost
Markup       = Gross profit ÷ (Offer + OH cost)
Margin       = Gross profit ÷ Sale price

Markup is the return on the cash you put in. Margin is the share of each sale you keep. They sound alike, and mixing them up changes which deals look good.

What the spreadsheets showed

Before building anything, I reverse-engineered the formulas in both deal workbooks.

The headline margin wasn't a margin

The team's go-to profit figure was profit divided by overhaul cost alone. It ignores the purchase price, so a part with a cheap overhaul and an expensive purchase could score well while tying up far more capital.

Quote sources vary by deal

93% vs 97%

In one deal, 93% of price points were direct supplier quotes. In the other, 97% were generic market listings. Same workbook layout, very different confidence in the numbers.

Copied formulas hide errors

A "most expensive" column was returning the same figure as a cheapest-price column, because a lookup had been copied across without changing its column index.

Headers drift between deals

The same column appeared as PN and Part number, and one tab name picked up a trailing full stop. Anything that reads these sheets has to match columns by name, not by position.

What I built

A web app that sits on the team's existing sheets. Nothing moves to a new system.

The solver

Five numbers describe a part's deal: sale price, OH cost, offer, markup and gross profit. Two equations link them, so any three decide the other two. The buyer ticks the three they know, and the tool solves the rest as they type, drag a slider or click a repair quote.

The buyer asksPin these threeTool solves
What's the most I can offer?Sale priceOH costMarkup %Offer, gross profit
The seller wants $X. What markup do I get?Sale priceOH costOfferMarkup, gross profit
What do I need to sell it for?OH costOfferMarkup %Sale price, gross profit
I need $Y profit on this part. What can I pay?Sale priceOH costGross profitOffer, markup

Ticking a fourth input releases the oldest one. Sale price, markup and gross profit together fix only the total cost, not how it splits between the offer and the overhaul, so the tool asks for OH cost or offer instead of guessing.

Review and hardening

A later review of my own delivered code found six issues. Two examples: the saved-prices view had no button to open it, and markup was being stored in a column labelled margin. All six are fixed in a v2. Columns are now matched by name across deal layouts, and locks survive a page reload.

The v2 comes with 40 automated checks: 16 against a mocked spreadsheet for the backend and 24 in a real browser on sample data.

Where it stands

There is no before-and-after timing yet. The team's baseline was six to nine hours per deal, and the target was about 90 minutes. The optimizer covers the pricing step: one screen per part instead of three tabs and a calculator. Collecting quotes from suppliers and repair stations is still done by hand.

The next step is to time the next live deal against that baseline.

What I'd build next

  • A deal-level view. Total capital needed, expected profit and return across every part, so the buyer can price the whole lot as well as each part.
  • Computed supply and demand classes. The GOLD, SILVER and BRONZE labels are typed by hand today. They can be derived from listing counts.
  • Quote freshness. A ten-year-old listing currently counts the same as last month's quote. Recent quotes should weigh more.
  • Automated quote collection. Send requests to suppliers and repair stations, then extract prices, lead times and certificates from their replies straight into the quote tabs.
